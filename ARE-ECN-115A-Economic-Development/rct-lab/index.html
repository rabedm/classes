How can we identify the causal impact of a policy?

Use this resource to define the causal effect of a policy, understand selection bias and see what random assignment accomplishes.

Begin with Miguel and Kremer (2004) ↓Read the published article ↗

Background

School-based deworming in western Kenya

Intestinal worms can harm children’s health and make regular school participation more difficult. Miguel and Kremer (2004) studied whether providing deworming medication through primary schools could improve student outcomes. In the example that follows, we focus on student attendance as the outcome.

The program was introduced across 75 schools in phases. We will use this setting to understand how researchers can separate what the policy caused from everything else affecting attendance.

01 · The fundamental problem of causal inference

Each school has two potential outcomes, but only one is observed.

The switching equation

Yi = Yi1Di + Yi0(1 − Di)

Yi=The observed attendance outcome

Yi1=Attendance if school i receives treatment

Yi0=Attendance if school i does not receive treatment

Every school has both potential outcomes. Treatment assignment Di determines which potential outcome is observed as Yi.

Why this is a problem

For the same school, we observe attendance with treatment or without treatment—but never both.

The unobserved potential outcome is the counterfactual. Without it, we cannot calculate the causal effect directly.

02 · Finding a control group

A control group provides an observed comparison.

We cannot observe the treated schools’ counterfactual outcome, so we compare them with another group. Whether that comparison recovers the treatment effect depends on the research design.

βˆ = E[Yi1 | Di=1] − E[Yi0 | Di=0]
We compare the observed outcome among treated schools with the observed outcome among untreated schools.

A · Cross-sectional comparison

Select schools that are already treated and untreated.

Suppose researchers select schools already providing deworming medication and compare them with schools that are not. The researchers do not randomly assign either group.

Does this comparison isolate the treatment effect?

Only if the untreated schools represent what would have happened to the treated schools without deworming medication. External factors related to both treatment status (Di) and attendance without treatment (Yi0) can make the groups different even before treatment enters the comparison, which introduces selection bias.

Positive selection bias

Family wealth

Students from wealthier families may attend schools that are more likely to already provide deworming medication. They may also attend school more regularly even without that medication. Because family wealth raises both Di and Yi0, it creates positive selection bias.

For this example, assume the treatment effect is 6.2 percentage points and the selection bias associated with family wealth is 5 percentage points.

The 6.2 percentage point treatment effect is the first-year estimate in Miguel and Kremer (2004), Table IX, regression 2. The selection-bias values are illustrative and are not estimates from the evaluation.

C · Randomized controlled trial

Use a random assignment mechanism.

Researchers begin with a sample of eligible schools and randomly assign each school to treatment or control. Baseline characteristics no longer determine treatment status systematically.
